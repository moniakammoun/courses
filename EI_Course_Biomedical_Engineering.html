Foundations of
Emotional Intelligence

Who am I emotionally? This module lays the theoretical and practical foundations of EI — the founding models, your strengths and areas for development, your overall vision of life.

📚 Salovey & Mayer (1990) 📚 Goleman (1995) 📖 Tolle — The Power of Now 📖 Ruiz — The Four Agreements
“Your brain is designed to keep track of all your emotions. Your job is to recognize them, not to suppress them.” — Daniel Goleman, Emotional Intelligence (1995)
Foundational theory

The models of Emotional Intelligence

The Salovey & Mayer model (1990) — The ability model

Peter Salovey and John Mayer were the first to define Emotional Intelligence scientifically as a set of cognitive abilities related to processing emotional information. This model, known as the “ability model”, identifies 4 hierarchical branches:

BRANCH 01
Perceiving emotions
Reading emotions in faces, voices, images and cultural expressions.
BRANCH 02
Using emotions
Harnessing emotional states to facilitate thinking and creativity.
BRANCH 03
Understanding emotions
Grasping emotional complexity — how emotions evolve and combine.
BRANCH 04
Managing emotions
Regulating your own emotions and positively influencing those of others.
Going further: Mayer, J. D., & Salovey, P. (1993). The intelligence of emotional intelligence. Intelligence, 17(4), 433–442. This model is the only one validated by objective performance tests (MSCEIT).
Daniel Goleman

Goleman's 5 components

Daniel Goleman (1995) popularized EI in his landmark book by proposing a mixed model that integrates personality traits and social skills. He identifies five key domains:

  1. Self-awareness — Recognizing your own emotions and their impact. This is the foundation of EI.
  2. Self-regulation — Controlling your impulses, adapting, not reacting in the heat of the moment. Directly linked to Ruiz's Four Agreements.
  3. Motivation — Committing for deep reasons, beyond external reward. Tolle calls this “acting from Being”.
  4. Empathy — Perceiving and understanding the emotions of others. Carnegie makes it the art of positive influence.
  5. Social skills — Managing relationships, communicating, inspiring and influencing.
Key quote: “Self-awareness — recognizing a feeling as it happens — is the keystone of emotional intelligence.” — Goleman (1995), p. 46

Link with “The Power of Now” (Tolle)

Tolle proposes that most of our emotional suffering comes from our identification with our thoughts rather than from observing them. EI begins with this ability to observe your own mental states without being swept away by them.

“Don't try to get rid of emotional pain. Let it be. Presence is the key to transformation.” — Eckhart Tolle, The Power of Now

Self-Awareness —
Observing without judging

Self-awareness is the ability to see your own emotions, thoughts and behaviors as a kind observer. This module combines Tolle's teachings on presence with validated mindfulness techniques.

📖 The Power of Now (Tolle) 🧠 Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) 📊 Goleman — Component 1
“The moment you realize you are not your thoughts is the moment you begin to awaken.” — Eckhart Tolle, The Power of Now
Theory

Mindfulness as a tool for self-awareness

Mindfulness is defined by Jon Kabat-Zinn (1994) as “paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgmentally, to the unfolding of experience moment by moment.”

Tolle reframes this idea from a more spiritual perspective: self-awareness begins when we stop identifying with the mind (the incessant stream of thoughts) and become the witness of that stream. This distinction between “the one who thinks” and “the one who observes the one who thinks” is fundamental.

The 3 levels of self-observation

  1. Level 1 — Physical: Feeling the physical sensations linked to the emotion (racing heart, muscle tension, heat).
  2. Level 2 — Emotional: Naming the emotion without amplifying or suppressing it.
  3. Level 3 — Mental: Observing the thoughts that accompany the emotion without taking them as absolute truths.
Key concept

Non-judgmental self-observation (Tolle)

Tolle distinguishes two fundamental states of consciousness:

  • Identification (unconscious): You are your thoughts, your emotions, your story. Emotional pain seems to be “you”.
  • Presence (conscious): You observe your thoughts and emotions. You are not the storm — you are the sky in which the storm takes place.
Practical application: The next time you feel an intense emotion, try asking yourself inwardly: “Who is observing this emotion?” This simple shift in perspective activates self-awareness.

The “Pain Body” according to Tolle: This is the accumulation of unresolved emotional residue. When activated, it seeks to feed on new pain. Self-awareness is the best antidote — you cannot be both conscious and reactive at the same time.

Self-Regulation —
Transforming reactions

How can you stop being a slave to your emotional reactions? This module explores emotional regulation tools — from the Four Agreements to cognitive behavioral therapy — to develop a conscious response rather than an automatic reaction.

📖 The Four Agreements (Ruiz) 🧠 Cognitive Behavioral Therapy — ABC Model (Ellis) 📊 Goleman — Component 2
“The happiness of your life depends upon the quality of your thoughts.” — Marcus Aurelius Reworded by Don Miguel Ruiz: “Be impeccable with your word.”
The Four Agreements — Ruiz

The Four Agreements: a code of emotional conduct

In The Four Agreements (1997), Don Miguel Ruiz proposes four principles drawn from the ancestral Nahuatl wisdom of Mexico. These agreements are concrete tools for self-regulation.

AGREEMENT 01
Be impeccable with your word
Speak with integrity. Say only what you really mean. Use the power of your word in the direction of truth and love.

Application in EI: Before speaking under the influence of anger, ask yourself: “Are these words true? Are they necessary? Are they kind?”
AGREEMENT 02
Don't take anything personally
What others say and do is a projection of their own reality. When you are immune to the opinions and actions of others, you no longer suffer needlessly.

Application in EI: Negative feedback speaks of the other person's perception, not of your intrinsic worth.
AGREEMENT 03
Don't make assumptions
Find the courage to ask questions and to express what you really want. Communicate clearly to avoid sadness, misunderstandings and drama.

Application in EI: In a conflict, start by checking your interpretation: “Do I have concrete evidence of this intention?”
AGREEMENT 04
Always do your best
Your “best” varies depending on the situation, the day, the energy available. By always doing your best, you avoid excessive self-criticism and regret.

Application in EI: Self-compassion is a component of emotional regulation — be kind to yourself.
Click on each card to see the practical application in Emotional Intelligence.
Neurobiology

Emotional regulation — What science says

Emotional regulation refers to all the processes by which we influence our emotions — when we have them, how we experience them, how we express them (Gross, 1998).

Regulation strategies

  • Cognitive reappraisal: Changing the way you interpret a situation to modify its emotional impact. The most effective according to research.
  • Expressive suppression: Repressing emotional expression. Effective in the short term, costly in the long term (cognitive rebound).
  • Acceptance (Tolle / Acceptance and Commitment Therapy): Welcoming the emotion without fighting or amplifying it.
  • Self-distancing: Observing the situation as if you were watching a film about yourself.
The response gap: Viktor Frankl put it this way: “Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space lies our freedom.” It is in this space that self-regulation lives.

Interpersonal Relationships —
The art of connecting

How do you create authentic connections? This module explores empathy, active listening and Nonviolent Communication through Carnegie's teachings, complemented by contemporary research on human relationships.

📖 How to Win Friends and Influence People (Carnegie) 💬 Nonviolent Communication (NVC) — Marshall Rosenberg 📊 Goleman — Components 4 & 5
“The sincere interest you take in others is the most important talent in communication.” — Dale Carnegie, How to Win Friends and Influence People (1936)
Theoretical foundation

Empathy — Much more than compassion

Empathy is often confused with sympathy. Brené Brown (2010) draws a fundamental distinction:

  • Sympathy: Looking at someone in a hole and telling them “Oh dear, that's terrible!” from up above.
  • Empathy: Climbing down into the hole and telling them “I know what it's like. You're not alone.”

The 3 types of empathy (Paul Ekman)

  • Cognitive empathy: Intellectually understanding the other person's perspective.
  • Affective empathy: Feeling the other person's emotions (risk of empathic distress).
  • Empathic concern (Compassionate Empathy): Feeling + acting to help. This is the most useful form in a professional context.
Neuroscience: Mirror neurons (Rizzolatti, 1996) are the neurobiological basis of empathy — they activate when we observe the actions and emotions of others, as if we were experiencing them ourselves.
Dale Carnegie — Fundamental principles

How to Win Friends and Influence People — The key principles

Published in 1936, How to Win Friends and Influence People remains one of the best-selling personal development books in history. Carnegie distills decades of observing human behavior into actionable principles:

  • P01
    Become genuinely interested in other people. People listen to you when they feel that you are really listening to them.
  • P02
    Smile. A smile is the universal shortcut to trust and connection.
  • P03
    Remember people's names. To a person, their name is the sweetest sound in any language.
  • P04
    Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.
  • P05
    Talk in terms of the other person's interests. The only way to positively influence someone is to talk about what they want.
  • P06
    Make the other person feel important — and do it sincerely. The desire to feel important is the deepest human urge.
Link with EI: These principles are the external face of Goleman's components 4 (Empathy) and 5 (Social skills). Carnegie had intuitively codified social EI before the concept even existed.
Nonviolent Communication

Marshall Rosenberg's NVC

Nonviolent Communication (NVC) is a model developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg in the 1960s. It offers a 4-component structure for authentic and effective communication.

O
Observation
Describe the concrete facts, without evaluation or judgment.
F
Feeling
Express what you feel (an emotion, not an opinion).
N
Need
Identify the unmet need behind the feeling.
R
Request
Make a concrete, positive and achievable request.

Example: Instead of “You never listen to me!” (judgment) → “When you look at your phone while I'm talking to you (O), I feel invisible (F), because I need to feel that what I say matters (N). Could you put it down during our conversation? (R)”

Social Intelligence & Leadership

Decoding the mechanisms of influence, managing conflicts intelligently and adopting the leadership style suited to each context.

📖 Cialdini — Influence 📖 Carnegie — How to Win Friends and Influence People ⏱ 1h30 🧠 Social intelligence
Influence & Persuasion
"The best way to persuade people is with your ears — by listening to them." — Dale Carnegie, How to Win Friends and Influence People

Cialdini's 6 Principles

In Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion (1984), Robert Cialdini identified six universal levers that automatically trigger compliance. Understanding these principles protects against their misuse and allows you to use them ethically.

1
Reciprocity
We feel obliged to give back what we receive. A gift creates a sense of psychological debt.
2
Commitment & Consistency
Once committed, we act consistently with our past statements, even if they no longer suit us.
3
Social proof
We watch others to decide how to act, especially under uncertainty. “Everyone does it” is a powerful trigger.
4
Authority
We trust experts and authority figures. Titles, uniforms and diplomas increase credibility.
5
Liking
We say yes more easily to people we like: attractiveness, similarity, sincere compliments.
6
Scarcity
The perceived value of something increases when it is rare or available for a limited time. Fear of missing out (FOMO) drives action.
Ethical application: Carnegie insists on authenticity — becoming genuinely interested in others, not manipulating them. Cialdini's principles describe human behavior; they are not recipes for manipulation.
Conflict Management

The Thomas-Kilmann Model

Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann (1974) modeled 5 conflict management styles along two axes: assertiveness (defending your own interests) and cooperativeness (satisfying the other person's interests). No style is inherently superior — the intelligent leader adapts their style to the context.

Collaborating
High assertiveness · High cooperativeness

Ideal for high-stakes issues. Seeks a win-win solution through in-depth dialogue. Takes time but creates lasting commitment.

Competing
High assertiveness · Low cooperativeness

Useful in emergencies or when non-negotiable values are at stake. Risks damaging the relationship if used systematically.

Accommodating
Low assertiveness · High cooperativeness

Appropriate when the issue matters little to you but a lot to the other person. Builds goodwill but can lead to resentment if systematic.

Avoiding
Low assertiveness · Low cooperativeness

Useful to let emotions cool down or when the conflict is trivial. Leaves problems unresolved if they are systematically avoided.

Compromising
Moderate assertiveness · Moderate cooperativeness

Each party gives something up. Quick and pragmatic. May leave both parties partly dissatisfied, but keeps the relationship working.

Leadership Styles

Goleman's 6 Styles (2002)

In Primal Leadership, Daniel Goleman and his co-authors identify six leadership styles, each relying on specific emotional competencies. The most effective leaders move fluidly from one style to another depending on the situation.

StyleApproachImpact on climateWhen to use it
Visionary“Come with me”Very positiveChange of direction, new vision
Coaching“Try this”PositiveLong-term development of team members
Affiliative“People come first”PositiveTensions, motivation after a crisis
Democratic“What do you think?”PositiveConsensus, new ideas
Pacesetting“Do as I do, now”Often negativeQuick results from a motivated team
Commanding“Do what I tell you”NegativeCrisis, absolute emergency only
Emotional intelligence and leadership: Goleman shows that IQ and technical skills explain access to leadership positions, but EI explains 80-90% of the performance gap between outstanding leaders and average leaders.

Motivation & the Personal Legend

Exploring the deep sources of lasting motivation, reaching states of flow and discovering your ikigai through Coelho's wisdom and positive psychology.

📖 Coelho — The Alchemist 📖 Csikszentmihalyi — Flow ⏱ 1h30 🧠 Intrinsic motivation
Self-Determination
"The most lasting motivation arises when people do things because they are meaningful, not because they are forced to." — Edward Deci & Richard Ryan, Self-Determination Theory

The 3 Basic Needs

Self-Determination Theory (SDT, Deci & Ryan, 1985) distinguishes intrinsic motivation — acting out of interest and enjoyment — from extrinsic motivation — acting for external rewards. It identifies three universal psychological needs whose satisfaction fuels lasting motivation and well-being.

🧭
Autonomy
Feeling that you are the source of your choices and actions. Acting in line with your values, without oppressive external pressure.
💪
Competence
Feeling effective, able to meet challenges that match your abilities. Progress fuels motivation.
🤝
Relatedness
Feeling connected to others, being part of a community, being seen and understood by your peers.
The motivation continuum: SDT describes a spectrum ranging from amotivation (total absence) to pure intrinsic motivation, through various forms of extrinsic regulation (external, introjected, identified, integrated). The goal is to gradually internalize motivations.
The State of Flow

The 8 Conditions of Flow — Csikszentmihalyi

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described flow (1990) as an optimal state of total immersion, where effort becomes invisible and time disappears. It arises from the perfect balance between the level of challenge and the level of skill.

  1. Clear goals — Knowing precisely what you are trying to achieve moment by moment.
  2. Immediate feedback — Continuously receiving information about your progress and adjustments.
  3. Challenge-skill balance — The challenge is slightly above your current level, stimulating without overwhelming.
  4. Merging of action and awareness — Actions become automatic and fluid, with no apparent effort.
  5. Total concentration — Attention is entirely absorbed by the task. Distractions disappear.
  6. Sense of control — A feeling of mastery and power without particular anxiety.
  7. Loss of self-consciousness — The ego temporarily fades into the activity, self-criticism falls silent.
  8. Altered sense of time — Hours feel like minutes, or the reverse depending on the nature of the activity.
Creating the conditions for flow: Identify the activities that trigger this state, structure your work to increase the challenge as your skills grow, and protect blocks of uninterrupted deep work.
The Personal Legend

The Alchemist and the Path of the Soul

In The Alchemist (1988), Paulo Coelho introduces the concept of the Personal Legend: each individual's own reason for being, the path their soul wishes to follow before being conditioned by fears and social expectations.

The 4 obstacles to the Personal Legend

  • Childhood: We are told our dreams are impossible or unrealistic. The first task is to rediscover this lost faith.
  • Love: The fear of hurting those we love by leaving them to follow our path. Coelho shows that true love supports the Legend.
  • Fear of failure: Defeats along the way seem like proof that we were wrong to dream. But every path requires mistakes.
  • Fear of realizing your dream: The most subtle: the fear of deserving your own happiness, of not being up to it.
Ikigai

Finding Your Reason for Being

A Japanese concept (生き甲斐, "reason for living"), Ikigai is the point of intersection between four dimensions. This practical tool helps identify a fulfilling vocation that is useful to the world.

What you love (Passion)
What you are good at (Mission)
What the world needs (Vocation)
What you can be paid for (Profession)

Financial and Emotional Intelligence

Understanding how our emotions influence our financial decisions, identifying our cognitive biases and building a healthy relationship with money and value.

📖 Kiyosaki — Rich Dad Poor Dad 📖 Kahneman — System 1 / System 2 ⏱ 1h30 🧠 Financial decisions
Kiyosaki — Rich Dad Poor Dad
"The rich acquire assets. The poor and middle class acquire liabilities that they think are assets." — Robert Kiyosaki, Rich Dad Poor Dad (1997)

Assets, Liabilities and the Cashflow Quadrant

Kiyosaki offers a simple but powerful definition: an asset puts money in your pocket, a liability takes money out. This definition overturns many popular beliefs (for Kiyosaki, a main residence is a liability because it generates expenses, not income).

Emotional intelligence applied to finance: Kiyosaki insists on mastering emotions — fear (which prevents investing) and greed (which pushes you to take excessive risks) are the two enemies of rational financial decision-making.

The Cashflow Quadrant

Kiyosaki identifies four positions in the economy, each with a specific mindset:

E — Employee
Security above all

Trades time for money. Seeks job security. Pays taxes first.

S — Self-employed
You are your own boss

Does everything themselves. If they don't work, they don't get paid. Identity closely tied to work.

B — Business owner
Systems and people

Owns a system that works for them. Delegates to and leads competent people.

I — Investor
Money works for you

Makes their money work. Passive income. The ultimate goal of financial independence.

Kahneman — Behavioral Economics

System 1 and System 2

In Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (Nobel Prize in Economics 2002) distinguishes two modes of thinking that govern our decisions, especially financial ones:

System 1 — Fast

  • Automatic, intuitive, emotional
  • Works without conscious effort
  • Relies on heuristics (mental shortcuts)
  • Source of cognitive biases
  • Reacts in milliseconds

System 2 — Slow

  • Deliberate, analytical, rational
  • Requires conscious effort
  • Can correct System 1
  • Tires easily (ego depletion)
  • Takes time and energy

The main financial cognitive biases

Confirmation bias
Seeking information that confirms our existing convictions about an investment.
Anchoring bias
Fixating on the first price heard as a reference, even if arbitrary.
Endowment effect
Overvaluing what we own simply because we own it.
Present bias
Preferring a small immediate reward to a large future reward.
Herd bias
Following the crowd in investment decisions (speculative bubbles).
Overconfidence
Overestimating your ability to predict markets or assess risks.
Loss Aversion

Prospect Theory — Kahneman & Tversky

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) showed that losing €100 causes about twice as much emotional pain as gaining €100 brings pleasure. This fundamental asymmetry — loss aversion — explains many irrational financial decisions.

Concrete implications:
  • We hold on to losing shares for too long (hoping to recover without realizing the loss)
  • We sell winning shares too early (fear of losing the gain)
  • We avoid profitable investments for fear of potential loss
  • We take excessive risks to recover a loss ("doubling down" behavior)
EI and loss aversion: Emotional awareness (Module 2) helps identify when it is fear — and not analysis — that drives a financial decision. Emotional regulation (Module 3) creates a space between emotion and action to activate System 2.

📖 Dyer — The Power of Intention ⏱ 1h30 🎓 Final module 📋 Synthesis + Action plan
The Power of Intention
"Intention is not something you do, but rather an energy you are connected to." — Wayne Dyer, The Power of Intention (2004)

The 7 Faces of Intention

Wayne Dyer redefines Intention not as mere willpower or an effort of will, but as a universal creative force that anyone can connect to. He describes seven qualities of this force, which every conscious individual can cultivate.

Face 1
Creativity
Intention is a creative force — everything that exists comes from it. We take part in creation when we act from this space.
Face 2
Kindness
Intention is essentially kind. Connecting to it means acting from kindness rather than fear.
Face 3
Love
Unconditional love is the highest vibration. Dyer asserts that Intention operates from love, not from fear or control.

Quick theoretical grounding

15 min

The 4 levels of listening (Otto Scharmer)

  • Level 1 — Downloading: we hear what we expect to hear, without really listening
  • Level 2 — Factual listening: we notice new facts, differences
  • Level 3 — Empathic listening: we move into the other person's world, we see from their perspective
  • Level 4 — Generative listening: we co-create something new that did not exist before

NVC (Nonviolent Communication, Rosenberg) offers a 4-step framework to move from level 1 to level 3:

O — Observation

Describe the facts without judgment or evaluation. “When I see / I hear...”

F — Feeling

Express what you really feel. “I feel...” (not “I feel that...”)

N — Need

Identify the unmet need behind the feeling. “Because I need...”

R — Request

Make a concrete, positive, negotiable request. “Would you be willing to... ?”

Obstacles to listening

Judging, advising, minimizing, comparing, interrogating, deflecting, reassuring too soon

Active listening posture

Eye contact, nodding, rephrasing, open questions, welcoming silence

Self-diagnosis: at what level do I listen?

Think of a recent difficult conversation. Which level of listening did you mainly use? What stopped you from going further?

2

NVC role plays in pairs

35 min

Form pairs. Each scenario lasts 7 minutes (role A 3 min, role B listens + responds 3 min, debrief 1 min), then switch roles. Read the scenario, do not prepare it in advance.

Scenario A — The overwhelmed colleague

Role A · The speaker
"I can't take it anymore. My manager has added three more urgent projects even though I had already said I was at full capacity. I feel like I'm not respected, like my opinion doesn't count. This morning I almost broke down in a meeting."
Role B · The listener
Your mission: practice the 4 levels. First welcome without judging, rephrase what you understood (feeling + need), then ask an open question. Give no advice before you have named the need.

Reflection after Scenario A

Scenario B — The strained relationship

Role A · The speaker
"My friend and I have barely spoken for weeks. The last time we saw each other, I said something that hurt them and since then there's been silence. I'd like to repair things but I don't know how to bring up the subject without making it worse."
Role B · The listener
Practice empathic listening (level 3). Resist the urge to give advice. Ask questions to explore feelings and needs. Finish with a complete O-F-N rephrasing before proposing a joint request.

Reflection after Scenario B

3

Group listening — The talking circle

25 min

Talking circle protocol

  • Form groups of 5 to 6 people seated in a circle
  • One person speaks at a time, without being interrupted (3 min timed)
  • The others listen in silence, without taking notes, without reacting verbally
  • After each turn, 30 seconds of collective silence
  • Then a single person may reflect what they heard (without interpreting)

Opening question for the circle (everyone answers):

"Which emotion or situation in my current life needs the most attention right now?"

Observations during the circle

  • I stayed silent and avoided interrupting for the entire time
  • I noticed my own thoughts arising (judgments, advice) without expressing them
  • My reflection described what I heard without adding interpretation
  • I felt a quality of connection different from ordinary conversation
